Simplified research strategies and designs.
Other methods may also be suitable depending on the research design. Explain your reasoning!

1. Qualitative Exploratory Research

Understand how customers experience a new product.

Preferred match: Interview customers about their experiences.

Interviews help explore customers' experiences in their own words.

2. Quantitative Survey Research

Measure customer satisfaction with a delivery service.

Preferred match: Use a structured satisfaction questionnaire.

A structured questionnaire collects comparable satisfaction ratings from many customers.

3. Experimental Research

Test whether a discount increases purchase intention.

Preferred match: Randomly assign customers to different discount conditions.

Random assignment and comparison between conditions help test causal effects.

4. Observational Field Research

Understand how shoppers behave inside a supermarket.

Preferred match: Observe and record shoppers' movements.

Direct observation captures shoppers' behavior in the actual setting.

5. Qualitative Document Analysis

Explore how a brand's advertising messages changed over time.

Preferred match: Analyze advertisements from different years.

Historical advertisements provide evidence of changes in brand messaging.

6. Mixed-Methods Research

Understand both how satisfied customers are and why they feel that way.

Preferred match: Combine satisfaction questionnaires with customer interviews.

Combining quantitative ratings and qualitative interviews addresses both measurement and explanation.

Deduction vs. Induction

Two different starting points for research.

Deduction

START WITH A THEORY

Theory↓Hypothesis↓Observations

Test an existing idea

Deduction

LOVE-TRIANGLE EXAMPLE

Theory

“Boyfriend” always means a romantic partner.

Hypothesis

Noé and Milo must be dating.

Observation

Ask Noé what he means by “boyfriend.”

Induction

START WITH OBSERVATIONS

Observations↓Patterns↓Theory

Develop an explanation

Induction

LOVE-TRIANGLE EXAMPLE

Observations

Noé uses “boyfriend” to mean a male friend.

Patterns

People use relationship labels differently.

Emerging Explanation

The meaning of “boyfriend” depends on context and interpretation.

Deduction: Theory → Evidence | Induction: Evidence → Theory

Induction or Deduction? You Decide!

Six marketing research scenarios. Can you identify the approach?

Scenario 1 of 6

Marketing Research Challenge

A. Deduction
B. Induction

All 6 Scenarios Completed!

Deduction tests ideas against evidence. Induction develops ideas from evidence.

Scenario 1 — Deduction

A researcher believes discounts increase purchase intention. They design an experiment to test this hypothesis.

The researcher starts with a hypothesis and collects evidence to test it.

Scenario 2 — Induction

A researcher interviews Gen Z consumers about sustainable fashion, identifies recurring themes, and develops an explanation of their purchasing motivations.

The researcher starts with observations and develops an explanation from emerging patterns.

Scenario 3 — Deduction

Based on an existing theory, a researcher predicts that influencer credibility increases brand trust and tests this using survey data.

The researcher derives a prediction from existing theory and tests it using evidence.

Scenario 4 — Induction

A researcher observes shoppers in a supermarket, notices unexpected buying patterns, and develops a new explanation for impulse purchases.

The researcher begins with observations, identifies patterns, and develops an explanation.

Scenario 5 — Deduction

A researcher hypothesizes that personalized emails generate higher click-through rates than generic emails and runs an A/B test.

The researcher tests a predefined hypothesis using an experiment.

Scenario 6 — Induction

A researcher analyzes customer reviews without predefined hypotheses, discovers recurring complaints, and develops an explanation of customer dissatisfaction.

The researcher identifies patterns in existing evidence before developing an explanation.

Research Domain

Consumer Behavior

The broad field of research.

Research Topic

The Influence of Winemaker Gender Cues on Consumer Evaluations of Wine

The general subject being studied.

Research Problem

It is unclear whether and how winemaker gender cues affect consumers' evaluations and purchasing decisions.

A specific issue or knowledge gap that needs investigation.

Research Question

The question the study aims to answer.

What Makes a Good Research Question?

Three key differences between stronger and weaker research questions.

Stronger Questions

Open-ended

Invite exploration and explanation.

Researchable

Can be investigated using accessible evidence.

Meaningful

Encourage deeper inquiry and analysis.

Weaker Questions

Too closed

Invite only a simple answer.

Hard to research

Lack accessible evidence.

Limited value

Offer little room for meaningful analysis.

Tip: Research questions often begin with how, why, what, or which. Always ask: “So what? Why does this question matter?”

Background Research: Explore → Identify → Refine

Why background research?

To understand what is already known, identify what is worth investigating, and develop a more focused research question.

01

Explore

What is already known?

02

Identify

What is missing or unclear?

03

Remember

You don't need a perfect research question at the beginning. Background research helps you improve it.

A clear research question then guides what you read, what data you collect, and what you investigate.
