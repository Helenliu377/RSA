RISK 1 · FABRICATION IN YOUR DATA

Your Survey May Be Answered by a Machine

99.8%

of 6,000 standard attention-check trials were passed by an autonomous AI respondent (Westwood, 2025).

This is an experimental attention-check pass rate, not the percentage of real surveys answered by AI.

Researchers debate how widespread AI-generated survey responses are in real-world data collection.

What the Agent Could Do

Maintain a consistent demographic profile, remember earlier answers, respond plausibly to survey questions, and potentially influence survey results.

What This Means for Your Thesis

Recruit participants through trusted channels, combine multiple data-quality checks, and report your procedures transparently. Never present synthetic responses as genuine human data.

Source: Westwood (2025), PNAS, 122(47), e2518075122.   Read Local PDF

Speaker notes

  • Study context: Westwood designed an autonomous synthetic respondent with a survey-interface layer, a reasoning engine, a demographic persona, and memory of earlier answers. OpenAI's o4-mini was used for the primary experiments. This is a capability demonstration under specified experimental conditions, not a population estimate of survey contamination.
  • Statistic verification: local PDF page 5, Section 2.2.1, and page 6, Figure 5A. Across 6,000 trials (20 standard attention-check questions repeated 300 times), the respondent made 10 errors; the reported pass rate is rounded to 99.8%. Methods Section 6.2 on page 10 explains that repeated data collection used the reasoning engine without the slower, human-mimicking front-end.
  • Logic puzzles and reverse-shibboleth probes: the agent handled logical-reasoning checks and could conceal its nonhuman capabilities. For reverse-shibboleth questions, evasion often meant declining a task rather than displaying superhuman accuracy. Do not describe all such probes as solved puzzles. See Sections 2.2.1-2.2.3, pages 5-6.
  • Poll outcomes: targeted instructions changed the synthetic respondent's answers in controlled demonstrations. Sections 2.3.1-2.3.3, pages 6-8, discuss how injected synthetic responses could influence poll estimates. This does not show that AI agents automatically seek out or attack real surveys, or establish the prevalence of such attacks.
  • Scholarly response: Rothschild, Barari, Buskirk, Gordon, and Hillygus (2026), Reply to Westwood: Questioning the Empirical Evidence that AI Survey Contamination is Real and Substantial, is a publicly posted scholarly letter/preprint. It questions definitions, benchmarks, and claims of demonstrated real-world impact. Distinguish evidence of capability, human use of AI assistance, autonomous synthetic responding, and measured prevalence. Do not call this response a peer-reviewed PNAS article. Source: https://osf.io/preprints/socarxiv/ykxwj_v1 ; author listing: https://soubhikbarari.com/writings/ .
  • Session 7 connection: plan recruitment, consent and confidentiality safeguards, multiple data-quality checks, exclusion rules, and transparent reporting before collecting survey responses. No single attention check guarantees human participation.
  • Synthetic samples versus genuine human respondents: explicitly identified synthetic data may be used for appropriate, transparently reported methodological purposes. It must never be presented as genuine human participant data. Keep this distinction separate from allegations of fabrication in the Westwood demonstration itself.

Speaker notes

  • Four-layer structure: distinguish legal obligations, research integrity guidance, publisher and journal requirements, and assessment-specific permissions. These sources have different roles; the slide is a map of requirements, not a claim that every rule applies identically in every research setting.
  • Research exclusions: Article 2(6) concerns AI systems or models developed and put into service exclusively for scientific research and development. Article 2(8) covers research, testing and development before market placement or putting into service, excluding testing in real-world conditions. Do not turn these provisions into a blanket exemption for any researcher using an existing AI tool. Other applicable Union law remains relevant. Verified against the Commission AI Act Service Desk Article 2: https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-2 .
  • Later amendments: the Commission's current AI Act Service Desk identifies its text as based on the EUR-Lex consolidation dated 27 July 2026, reflecting the Digital Omnibus on AI. Its Article 2 retains the research exclusions and marks changes elsewhere. Recheck the current consolidated regulation and the particular provision before teaching detailed obligations or implementation dates; no such timeline is asserted on this slide.
  • Integrity and publication: ALLEA provides a research-integrity framework; Commission guidance addresses responsible generative AI use. COPE's position denies AI tools authorship and keeps responsibility with human authors. Check the individual journal's disclosure and AI-use policy too.
  • Source access: the supplied Commission guidelines landing URL is retained in the source panel. The current official document page is https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/document/2b6cf7e5-36ac-41cb-aab5-0d32050143dc_en . Some official sites may require browser verification or restrict automated access.
  • KEDGE: no official school policy URL has been supplied or inferred. Introduce the actual course requirements on the following slide; do not describe the placeholder as a verified institutional policy.
  • Edition verification (6 November 2026): ALLEA 2023 revised edition; COPE position last reviewed 13 February 2023; European Commission, Living guidelines on the responsible use of generative AI in research, fourth version, October 2026. KEDGE's Charter in Five Rules

01

Forbidden by default

Generative AI is banned in every assessment and exam unless the course leader (RCM) authorises it explicitly, in writing.

02

Declare every use

When you submit, state which tools you used, for what role, and how.

03

Keep a log

Prompts, sources checked, decisions, intermediate versions, your own contribution. It can be requested at any time.

04

Protect data

Never enter personal data, confidential information or unpublished research into an AI tool. Anonymise first.

05

Respect copyright

Never put copyrighted content or non-free data into a prompt.

In every case, you alone are responsible for the final content: verify, correct, validate.

Source: KEDGE Business School (2026), Research Skills, Method & IA syllabus 2027–2028, pp. 8–9 (charter referring to annexe 5). Applicability to 2026–2027 remains unconfirmed.

Two Different Consequences If You Cross the Line

THE TEACHER'S CALL

Academic appraisal

If your work or a missing or thin AI log does not show your personal contribution, the teacher can judge it unassessable, down to 0/20. No misconduct needs to be proven.

PROGRAMME MANAGEMENT

Disciplinary procedure

Unauthorised AI use, fraud or a deliberate breach is reported. The disciplinary board can issue a blame, a temporary exclusion of up to two years, or a permanent exclusion.

Plagiarism is fraud too

Copying or paraphrasing without a source, reusing others' data or another student's work. Copyright infringement can carry penalties of up to 3 years in prison and a €300,000 fine under French law. KEDGE uses automated checks on submitted work.

Sources: KEDGE syllabus 2027–2028, pp. 7–9; French Intellectual Property Code, arts. L. 335-2–L. 335-3.

Can AI Make Your Research Better?

A simple example of AI-assisted academic writing

Original Writing

Master's Student

Influencer marketing is widely used in the beauty industry, especially by retailers like Sephora. Many young consumers watch beauty influencers on TikTok and Instagram before buying products. These influencers can affect consumer trust and purchase decisions, but their impact may depend on how credible consumers find them.

AI-Refined Writing

Perplexity

Influencer marketing is widely adopted in the beauty industry, particularly by retailers such as Sephora. Young consumers frequently engage with beauty-related content on TikTok and Instagram before making purchase decisions. While beauty influencers can influence consumer trust and purchase intentions, the magnitude of their impact may vary with consumers’ perceptions of their credibility.

What Did AI Actually Improve?

Did it improve the research itself, or just the way the research sounds?

More fluent writing ≠ stronger research.
